\section{Task 3: Optimal Operational Decision-Making Framework for Water Treatment Plants}
\subsection{Task Background and Core Modeling Approach}
Task 3 serves as the decision-making phase in the "prediction-decision" closed-loop system, directly building on the contaminant concentration prediction results from Task 2. The core objective of this task is to formulate an optimal 24-hour shutdown/operation scheduling plan for water treatment plants, achieving a balance among operational stability, water supply reliability, and public health risks by minimizing a comprehensive weighted cost function.

To address the temporal dependency of hourly decisions (where decisions at each time step affect subsequent states and costs), this study adopts a two-tier modeling approach that combines an optimization model with a solution algorithm to ensure scientific and optimal decision-making:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Weighted Multi-Objective Optimization Model}: As the core optimization carrier, it quantifies the trade-offs among different cost dimensions.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Programming Algorithm}: A solution algorithm designed based on the core idea of dynamic programming, used to solve time-varying optimal control problems, decompose global decision-making challenges, and ensure globally optimal decisions over the 24-hour time horizon.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Weighted Multi-Objective Cost Function}
The core of the model is a weighted sum of three mutually independent cost components, with weight design reflecting the priority ranking of operational stability, water supply reliability, and public health:
\begin{equation}
\min \quad \text{Total Cost} = \alpha \cdot C_{\text{shutdown}} + \beta \cdot C_{\text{supply loss}} + \gamma \cdot C_{\text{risk}}
\label{eq:total_cost}
\end{equation}
where normalized weights $\alpha=0.35$, $\beta=0.25$, and $\gamma=0.40$ prioritize public health risk prevention and control, followed by operational costs and water supply reliability.

\subsubsection{Shutdown Cost}
This cost component accounts for direct operational expenses incurred during plant shutdowns:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{shutdown}} = \sum_{t=0}^{23} C_s \cdot U(t)
\label{eq:shutdown_cost}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item $U(t) \in \{0,1\}$: Binary decision variable at time $t$ (1 = shutdown, 0 = operation);
    \item $C_s$: Hourly shutdown cost of the water plant (specific value given in the problem statement);
    \item The summation only accumulates costs during plant shutdown periods, aligning with the economic laws of actual operations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Supply Loss Cost}
This cost component quantifies the economic and social impacts of unmet water demand:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{supply loss}} = \sum_{t=0}^{23} C_l \cdot \max\left(D(t) - Q(t) \cdot (1-U(t)), \, 0\right)
\label{eq:supply_cost}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item $D(t)$/$Q(t)$: Water demand/supply rate at time $t$ (unit: $\text{m}^3$);
    \item $\max(\cdot,0)$: Only penalizes positive water supply gaps (demand exceeding supply), avoiding non-physical negative costs;
    \item $C_l$: Unit cost of supply loss (reflecting the value of unmet water demand for residents and industries).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Risk Cost (Concentration-Dependent Penalty Term)}
This cost component incorporates health risk penalties linked to contaminant concentrations, realizing quantitative trade-offs between water supply guarantee and public safety:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{risk}} = \sum_{t=0}^{23} \left[ k \cdot \max\left(C(t) - C_{\text{safe}}, \, 0\right) \right] \cdot Q(t) \cdot (1-U(t))
\label{eq:risk_cost}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item $C(t)$: Contaminant concentration (prediction result from Task 2, unit: mg/L);
    \item $C_{\text{safe}}$: Regulatory safety concentration threshold;
    \item $k \cdot \max\left(C(t) - C_{\text{safe}}, \, 0\right)$: Linear penalty for concentrations exceeding safety limits (risk proportional to concentration);
    \item $Q(t) \cdot (1-U(t))$: Risk cost is only calculated during plant operation periods (no contaminated water supply during shutdowns, hence no risk cost).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dynamic Programming Algorithm Design}
Based on Bellman's principle of optimality and the core idea of dynamic programming, a solution algorithm is designed to decompose the 24-hour time horizon into sequentially solvable subproblems, efficiently solve time-varying optimal control problems, and avoid the complexity of global decision-making.

\subsubsection{State Definition}
The state vector at time $t$ is defined as $V(t, C(t), S(t), U(t-1))$, representing the minimum cumulative cost from time $t$ to 23 under the following conditions:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item Contaminant concentration $C(t)$ (output from Task 2);
    \item Emergency water storage volume $S(t)$ (unit: $\text{m}^3$);
    \item Previous shutdown decision $U(t-1)$ (capturing operational hysteresis effects).
\end{itemize}
This state definition satisfies the Markov property (future states depend only on the current state), providing a core prerequisite for solving the dynamic programming algorithm and complying with the algorithm's optimality conditions.
